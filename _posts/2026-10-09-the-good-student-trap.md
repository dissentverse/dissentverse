“Is it really you?”

“I didn’t expect this from you.”

You must have heard these words at school, college, work, or even at home.

Maybe someone said them to you because you’ve always been considered obedient, well-mannered, and, overall, a good student. And now, you’ve done something they didn’t expect from you.\
Seems normal, right?\
But is it?

We all know how a good student is expected to act.

Obedient, disciplined, well-mannered. Someone who completes assignments on time, respects teachers and parents, follows instructions, and gets good marks. Teachers praise them, parents trust them, and they gradually become examples for others.

There is nothing wrong with any of this. Discipline, hard work, and respect are valuable, and listening to people with more experience often helps us. **The problem is not being a good student. The problem begins when you feel you cannot be anything else.**

From an early age, students learn which behaviours earn appreciation: following instructions, finishing work, performing well. Naturally, they want to be valued by the people whose opinions matter to them. **But over time, being a good student stops being something they do and becomes who they are.** The student who gets good marks becomes the one who always excels. The one who follows instructions becomes the one who never questions anyone.

**That is where the trap begins. When being a good student becomes part of who you are, doing something differently can feel like risking people’s approval.** You’re no longer afraid of just failing, but also of disappointing those who have always seen you as a good, sincere, and responsible student.

Take a student who has always been praised for being hardworking. One day they struggle, get lower marks than usual, or hesitate to speak up because they are afraid of looking incapable. For some, that is just a disappointing day. For this student, it feels like a verdict: their teachers might be disappointed, their parents might think differently of them, and perhaps they are no longer the person everyone believed them to be. **An ordinary setback turns into a failure to live up to an identity, making them stay silent rather than risk questioning a teacher or seeming disobedient.**

**Sometimes people follow expectations because they truly agree with them. Other times they follow them out of fear. From the outside, the two look identical. The difference is whether the person feels free to choose otherwise.** So the question that matters is this:

**Am I making this choice because I genuinely want to, or because I am afraid of disappointing someone?**

This becomes even more important when students have to make big decisions about their future. Imagine a student who wants to pursue a career their parents don’t want them to choose. They have always listened to their parents and tried to make them proud. Now, choosing a different career feels like disappointing them. They worry that their parents might be upset or think they have changed. Because of this fear, they may give up on what they really want and choose a career just to keep their parents happy.

**And the pattern doesn’t end with school. Expectations change, but the habit stays the same.** Someone who spent years being appreciated for following instructions grows into the employee who constantly seeks the manager’s approval, the friend who never disappoints anyone, the person who says yes when they want to say no. **They have learned to equate being good with being agreeable.** The student who feared disappointing a teacher becomes the adult who fears disappointing a parent, a partner, or a boss.

**And when making others proud becomes the main measure of doing well, it gets hard to recognise what doing well means to you.**

You may become so focused on meeting everyone’s expectations that you forget to ask yourself a simple question: What do I actually want?

**Being independent doesn’t mean ignoring others’ opinions or refusing to listen to advice. It means reclaiming your own autonomy** , being able to think for yourself, make your own choices, and understand that disagreeing with someone doesn’t mean you disrespect them.

You can value the people in your life without letting their expectations decide every part of yours. You can appreciate their advice while recognising that, sometimes, you may want something different. **And you can make a choice that disappoints someone without automatically making that choice wrong.**

Go back to that sentence from the beginning: “I didn’t expect this from you.”

Perhaps the problem isn’t that people are surprised when we do something different. **It’s that we can become so used to meeting their expectations that we start feeling guilty whenever we don’t.**

And in trying so hard to be the person everyone admires, we may forget that we’re allowed to grow, change our minds, and choose a path of our own.

**Being a good student should be something we choose to be, not something we’re afraid to stop being.**
