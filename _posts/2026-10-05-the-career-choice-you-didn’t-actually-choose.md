What do you want to become?\
Seems like a simple question.\
Maybe it is asked by parents, teachers, relatives or friends. It sounds simple, but it is not as simple as we think.\
At a certain point, not everyone knows what they want to become. Yet, somehow, they still manage to answer.\
***A doctor. An engineer. A teacher. A CA. A lawyer. A businessman.***\
*But where did that answer come from?*

\
Maybe their parents told them, *“Beta, tumhe doctor banna hai.”*\
Or maybe their teacher said, *“You are good at Maths and Physics. You should try JEE. You can get into an IIT, a highly prestigious college.”*\
Or maybe their friends were choosing the same path.\
*“Jo mera dost karega, main bhi wahi karunga.”*

\
Seems normal?\
Maybe it is. But it raises a question:

![](https://dissentverse.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/img-20260919-wa0009-edited.jpg?w=1024)

\
***How much of your career choice is actually yours?***

\
For many students, what feels like a personal career choice is actually shaped by the people and circumstances around them.\
It often begins at home. Parents naturally play an important role in shaping how their children think about their future. They may influence their children because they want the best for them and want them to have a secure future. They may prefer careers they consider stable or financially secure. The intention may not be wrong, but sometimes that expectation becomes stronger than the student’s own interest.

\
Then there are teachers. They can help students understand their abilities and guide them towards different career options. But sometimes, a teacher may suggest a particular career simply because a student performs well in a certain subject. Being good at Maths, for example, does not automatically mean that a student wants a career related to Maths.\
*Ability and interest are not always the same thing.*

\
As students grow older, their choices can also be shaped by their friends and classmates. When everyone around them seems to be moving in the same direction, choosing the same path can feel easier than figuring everything out on their own. Sometimes, a student may follow their friends without even knowing much about the career they are choosing.

\
And today, these influences do not come only from the people around us. Social media constantly shows students how others are studying, which careers they are pursuing and what success looks like. High salaries, prestigious colleges and stories of people achieving their goals appear again and again. Over time, these things can shape what students believe a successful career is supposed to look like.

\
Then there is the question of money and financial security. There is nothing wrong with considering them. A student may genuinely be interested in one career but choose another because it seems more financially rewarding, stable or respected by society.

\
So career choices are rarely shaped by just one thing. They develop gradually through what students hear at home, what they learn from teachers, what their friends are doing, what they see online and what society considers successful.

\
But the real problem begins when all these influences become louder than the student’s own voice.

\
When a student starts struggling with the career they have chosen, *they may not immediately question the choice itself.*

\
*They question themselves.*

\
When they repeatedly fail, they may think they did not work hard enough. They may have grown up hearing that if you work hard, success will follow. So failure starts feeling like proof that something is wrong with them.

\
They may feel guilty for not fulfilling their parents’ expectations or for disappointing the people who believed in them. And when they see their friends moving ahead, comparison adds another layer of pressure.

\
But there is one question they may not ask:\
**Did I ever really choose this career for myself?**

\
And this does not happen only when a student is failing.

\
Sometimes, a student can be doing well, getting good marks and meeting everyone’s expectations, but still feel that this is not the life they actually want.

\
Everyone around them may see success.\
But the student may still be questioning the path.

\
And that can be difficult to acknowledge when, from the outside, everything seems to be going well. There may be no obvious failure or clear reason to stop. They may simply feel that this is not what they want.

\
So how do they tell others that they don’t want what they have been working towards?

\
Sometimes, they may not even know how to explain it themselves.

\
And even if they do realise that they want something different, changing the path is not always easy. They may have already spent years preparing for that career and invested time, money and effort into reaching that point. Leaving it behind can feel like wasting everything they have already put into it.

\
Then comes the fear of being judged.\
They may think, “If I change my path, my parents will feel ashamed in front of relatives and society.”

\
Changing direction may feel like failure. And after spending years preparing for one career, starting something completely different can feel like it is too late, unfamiliar and uncertain.\
So continuing on the same path can seem easier than admitting that maybe it was never the right path for them.

\
But this brings us to another question:\
**If changing direction is seen as failure, should we continue on the same path simply because we have already spent years on it?**\
Maybe the problem is not always that a student made the wrong career choice.\
Maybe the problem is that they never got enough space to question that choice in the first place.\
We often ask students what they want to become, but rarely ask them **why they want to become it.**

\
We ask about marks, colleges, packages and future security. But how often do we ask **whether the student actually understands the path they are choosing?**

\
A career decision is rarely made by one person alone. It can be shaped by family, teachers, friends, money, social expectations and the idea of success that surrounds us.\
None of these influences are necessarily wrong.

\
But when all of them become louder than the student’s own voice, it becomes difficult to know where their choice ends and someone else’s influence begins.

\
And perhaps that is where the real question lies.\
**What if a student spends years trying to succeed in a career they never truly chose?**\
**Would their struggles always mean they did not work hard enough?**\
**Or could it sometimes mean that we never stopped to ask whether they were walking in the right direction?**

\
***Maybe changing direction is not always about giving up. Maybe sometimes it is simply about questioning where we are going before spending even more time getting there.***

\
So the next time when we ask a student, “What do you want to become?”, perhaps the more important question is:\
**“Is this what you actually want, or is this what you have been taught to want?”**
