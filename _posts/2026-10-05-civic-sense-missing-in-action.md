###### Progess isn’t just infrastucture – It’s behaviour

We often compare India with other countries in terms of facilities, infrastructure, wealth, opportunities, and development. Yet, there is one aspect we almost always overlook – our behaviour as citizens. We want world-class roads, clean cities, and disciplined systems, but rarely ask whether we contribute to these systems responsibly. Without responsible citizens, even the best infrastructure fails to serve its purpose.

While we admire how foreign countries maintain neat, organized, and disciplined public spaces, the reality in India is starkly different. People spit on walls, litter streets, urinate in public, damage property, break traffic rules, play loud music at night, misbehave with others, and often act as if public spaces belong to no one. This careless behaviour extends even to protests or strikes, where public property is destroyed,  buses burned, benches broken, walls vandalized and forgetting that these facilities are meant for everyone.

This leads to an important question: **what exactly is civic sense?** Simply put, it means **behaving responsibly in public spaces** **, respecting rules, other people, and shared resources. Civic sense is about more than following laws but it is about awareness, empathy, and active responsibility for the society we live in.**

Unfortunately, the gap between awareness and action is wide. Many people do not even know what civic sense is. For some, littering, spitting, or breaking rules is considered “normal behaviour.” And those who understand it often treat it as optional rather than essential. This ignorance, combined with careless habits, creates a bigger problem for society than inadequate infrastructure ever could.

And yet, we complain:\
*“The government doesn’t maintain roads… The city is dirty… Systems don’t work here.”*

But before blaming the system, we need to ask ourselves: how many of us take care of the facilities already provided? How many of us use them responsibly? Yes, governments have responsibilities, but citizens must share that responsibility. Development is never one-sided; it is a joint effort between people and the system. 

Civic sense is not only about  rules or laws . it is about empathy, awareness, and respect for others and shared spaces. A person with empathy will not spit on a wall because they know someone has to repaint it. They will not throw garbage on the road because someone else must clean it. They will not play loud music late at night because someone nearby may be resting, studying, or unwell. But  people care only when the problem affects them personally.

This lack of empathy is at the root of many societal problems. Civic sense is not just about punishment or regulation, it is about taking personal responsibility for the community we live in. Public spaces, roads, and facilities are meant for everyone, and maintaining them is a duty shared by citizens and the government alike.True progress requires citizens to act with empathy, think of the collective good, and take small, consistent actions to maintain public spaces.

It is our responsibility to act consciously every day: keeping our surroundings clean, protecting public property, following rules, and behaving considerately toward others. Progress cannot happen if we rely solely on the government; each citizen must take initiative to improve society, the state, and the nation.

India has incredible culture, diversity, talent, and potential. But true progress will begin only when we stop blaming the system for everything and start examining our own behaviour. Clean cities, safe roads, and peaceful communities are not created by speeches or complaints but they are built by citizens who care enough to act responsibly.

Ultimately, civic sense is **a shared duty, rooted in awareness, empathy, and collective responsibility**. Development is not measured by infrastructure alone, but by how well citizens uphold the values and behaviours that make a society functional, safe, and respectful.
