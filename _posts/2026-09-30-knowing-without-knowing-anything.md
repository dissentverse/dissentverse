You may be familiar with AI, the internet, data, GDP and inflation. You recognise these terms. You have probably heard them countless times and may even use some of them in your daily life.

But if someone suddenly asked you to explain one of them without looking it up, could you?

The answer might be different from what you expect.

Because sometimes, **we don’t actually know something , we just recognise it.**

And the more often we encounter an idea, the more familiar it becomes. Eventually, that familiarity can start feeling like understanding.

We have lots of information to consume. It may be an Instagram reel, a YouTube video, a podcast, an informational video, or maybe a lecture.

We consume information on a daily basis, from early morning to late at night, on different topics such as AI, politics, sports, economics and many others.

But how much of what we consume do we actually understand?

You can watch ten reels in just five minutes and come across ten different ideas. But if someone asks you about one of them later, how much can you actually remember? And if you remember it, can you explain it in your own words?

We often think, “I have heard this term, so I know it.”

But **knowing something and understanding it are not always the same thing.**

Knowing something can mean recognising a term, remembering a fact or repeating an explanation you have heard before.

Understanding requires more. It means knowing how or why something works, connecting it to other ideas, questioning it and being able to explain it in your own words.

Think about some of the things we come across regularly.

You know what inflation is, but can you explain how it occurs?

You use AI and know how to write prompts, but do you actually know how AI works or how it is trained?

You know GDP is commonly used when talking about an economy, but do you understand what it actually measures?

You use money every day, but do you know how money is created?

You may have heard the word feminism countless times or even identify with the idea, but could you explain what feminism actually means and why different people understand it differently?

These questions do not mean that you know nothing. They show how easy it is to become familiar with an idea without developing a deeper understanding of it.

And this can happen simply because we encounter the same ideas again and again.

We hear a term in a reel. Then we see it in a post. Someone mentions it in a podcast. We come across it again in a conversation.

Eventually, it stops feeling unfamiliar.

When we see it again, we think, “I know this.”

But sometimes, what we really know is that we have seen it before.

That familiarity can make us feel more confident about a subject than we actually are. The words sound familiar, the idea seems recognisable, and we assume we understand it.

Then someone asks a simple question:

“What does it actually mean?”

“Why does it happen?”

“How does it work?”

And suddenly, we realise that recognising something was never enough to explain it.

And this is also visible in the way we consume information today. We don’t just watch, read or listen to it. **We save it.**

We save posts, take screenshots, bookmark articles, save videos and add things to watch-later lists. There is nothing wrong with doing this. These can be useful ways of keeping information we genuinely want to return to.

But sometimes, saving information can create the feeling that we have already learned something simply because we have decided to keep it.

And the amount of information available to us makes this easier than ever.

We can search almost anything within seconds. We can ask AI to explain a complicated idea, watch a short video about it or read a summary instead of going through something in depth.

That access is valuable. The problem is not that information has become easier to find.

The problem begins when **being exposed to information starts feeling like understanding it.**

Maybe the question isn’t whether we know enough.

Maybe it is how much of what we think we know we could actually explain, question and connect to something else without simply repeating what we have already heard.

Because there is a difference between having encountered an idea and actually understanding it.

And perhaps the next time we think, **“I know this,”** we should stop for a moment and ask:

**Do I actually understand it or have I just seen it enough times to feel like I do?**
